The definite integral & the Fundamental Theorem

Differentiating and integrating are opposites. This theorem is why antiderivatives can find areas.

The idea

Define the accumulation function A(x) = ∫ax f(t) dt: the signed area from a up to x. Slide x to the right and the area grows at a rate equal to the height of f at the edge. That is FTC Part 1: A′(x) = f(x). Integration undoes differentiation.

FTC Part 2 turns that into a method: if F is any antiderivative of f, then ∫ab f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a). No more adding rectangles. A related fact: the net change of a quantity equals the integral of its rate, ∫ab f′(x) dx = f(b) − f(a).

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Choose t² − 2t and slide x to 2. What is happening to A?

    What you should see

    A is at its lowest point (A(2) = −4/3): f is negative until x = 2 (area below the axis counts negative), so A decreases, and f(2) = 0 where A turns around.

  2. Compare f(x) with the slope of A at several x. What do you see?

    What you should see

    They are equal everywhere: the slope of the accumulation graph is the height of f. That is FTC Part 1.

  3. On t² − 2t, move the start a from 0 to 1. What happens to the A graph, and what stays the same?

    What you should see

    The whole A graph moves up by 2/3 (it now crosses 0 at x = 1, because A(1) = 0), but its shape and its slope at every x are unchanged: A′(x) = f(x) whatever the start. Changing a only adds a constant. That is why any antiderivative works in FTC Part 2.

  4. Choose e^(−t²) and slide x to 3. Read A(3). Why is this function special?

    What you should see

    A(3) ≈ 0.8862, almost exactly √π/2 (the rest of the tail beyond 3 adds only about 0.00002). e^(−t²) has no antiderivative made of elementary functions, so A(x) = ∫0x e^(−t²) dt is a genuinely new function, and FTC Part 1 still tells you its derivative: A′(x) = e^(−x²).

Worked example

Evaluate ∫13 (2x + 1) dx, and find d/dx ∫0x² sin t dt.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Antiderivative. F(x) = x² + x.
  2. FTC Part 2. F(3) − F(1) = (9 + 3) − (1 + 1) = 10. The integral is 10.
  3. Second problem: chain rule. The upper limit is g(x) = x², so d/dx ∫0x² sin t dt = sin(x²)·(x²)′.
  4. Answer. 2x sin(x²).

Worked example 2: a water tank (AP-style)

Water flows into a tank at the rate E(t) = 6t − t² liters per hour and drains out at a constant 4 liters per hour, for 0 ≤ t ≤ 6 hours. At t = 0 the tank holds 50 liters. Let W(t) be the amount of water in the tank at time t. (a) How much water is in the tank at t = 6? (b) Is the amount of water increasing or decreasing at t = 3? At what rate? (c) At what time in [0, 6] is the amount of water least? Justify your answer.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Amount = start + ∫ net rate. The net rate is in minus out, so W(t) = 50 + ∫0t (6s − s² − 4) ds.
  2. (a) ∫06 (6t − t² − 4) dt = [3t² − t³/3 − 4t]06 = 108 − 72 − 24 = 12, so W(6) = 50 + 12 = 62 liters.
  3. (b) By FTC Part 1, W′(t) = E(t) − 4, so W′(3) = 9 − 4 = 5. The amount is increasing at 5 liters per hour.
  4. (c) Critical points. W′(t) = 0 when 6t − t² = 4, that is t² − 6t + 4 = 0, so t = 3 ± √5 ≈ 0.764 and 5.236. W′ < 0 before 0.764, W′ > 0 between, and W′ < 0 after 5.236.
  5. Candidates test. W(0) = 50, W(0.764) ≈ 48.546, W(5.236) ≈ 63.454, W(6) = 62. The least amount is about 48.546 liters, at t = 3 − √5 ≈ 0.764 hours: at first water drains faster than it flows in.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to subtract: F(b) − F(a), not F(b) + F(a).
  • Adding + C in a definite integral. It cancels, so leave it out.
  • Forgetting the chain rule factor g′(x) when the upper limit is not just x.
  • Treating area below the axis as positive. The integral is net (signed) area.
  • Swapping limits without changing the sign: ∫ba = −∫ab.
  • In amount problems, forgetting the starting amount, or using the inflow rate alone instead of in minus out.

Properties worth knowing

∫aa f = 0; ∫ab f = −∫ba f; ∫ac f = ∫ab f + ∫bc f; constants pull out and sums split. If f is the rate of change of a quantity, ∫ab f is the total change in that quantity from a to b.
