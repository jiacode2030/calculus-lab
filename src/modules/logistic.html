Logistic models

Real populations do not grow forever. A logistic model grows fast when small, slows as it approaches a carrying capacity, and levels off.

The idea

dP/dt = kP(1 − P/L). When P is small the factor (1 − P/L) is near 1, so growth is almost exponential. As P approaches the carrying capacity L, the factor shrinks to 0 and growth stops. P = 0 and P = L are equilibria; L is stable. The solution is an S-shaped curve with limit L.

The growth rate dP/dt is a parabola in P: zero at P = 0 and P = L, and largest at P = L/2, where the S-curve has its inflection point and steepest slope. The maximum rate is kL/4. Everything here (limit, max rate, inflection) can be read from the differential equation without solving it.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Set P₀ = 1, L = 8. When is the population growing fastest?

    What you should see

    When P = L/2 = 4 (the steepest part of the S, and the top of the parabola below). The maximum rate is kL/4 = 0.5·8/4 = 1.

  2. Now raise k to 1. What changes, and what does not?

    What you should see

    The fastest rate doubles to kL/4 = 1·8/4 = 2, and the S-curve happens in half the time. But growth is still fastest at P = 4, and the limit is still L = 8: k sets the speed, L sets where it ends.

  3. Start exactly at half the carrying capacity: P₀ = 4, L = 8. Where is the inflection point?

    What you should see

    There is none after t = 0. The population starts at the fastest-growth point, so the rate only falls from there and the curve is concave down the whole time.

  4. Start small: P₀ = 0.2, L = 8, and set t = 2. Compare P with the exponential 0.2ekt.

    What you should see

    The model gives P(2) ≈ 0.521; pure exponential growth would give 0.2e ≈ 0.544. While P is small, (1 − P/L) is close to 1, so logistic growth starts out nearly exponential. Later the two separate completely.

  5. Start P₀ above L (try P₀ = 10, L = 8). What does P do?

    What you should see

    It decreases toward L: (1 − P/L) is negative, so the model pulls the population back down to the carrying capacity.

Worked example

dP/dt = 0.02P(1 − P/500), P(0) = 50. Find limt→∞ P, the P at which growth is fastest, and the fastest rate.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Compare with kP(1 − P/L). k = 0.02, L = 500.
  2. Limit. The carrying capacity: lim P = 500 (since 0 < P(0) < L).
  3. Fastest growth. At P = L/2 = 250.
  4. Rate. kL/4 = 0.02(500)/4 = 2.5 per time unit.
  5. Answer. Limit 500; fastest at P = 250 with rate 2.5.

Worked example 2: stocking a lake (AP-style)

A lake is stocked with 200 fish. The population P(t), t years later, satisfies dP/dt = 0.4P(1 − P/5000). (a) Find limt→∞ P(t). (b) For what value of P is the population growing fastest, and how fast? (c) Is the graph of P concave up or concave down when P = 1000? Explain. (d) The solution is P(t) = 5000/(1 + 24e−0.4t). When does the population reach 2500?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. (a) Here k = 0.4 and L = 5000. Since 0 < 200 < 5000, lim P = 5000 fish.
  2. (b) dP/dt is largest at P = L/2 = 2500, where dP/dt = 0.4(2500)(1 − 1/2) = 500 fish per year.
  3. (c) Differentiate the equation: d²P/dt² = 0.4(1 − 2P/5000)·dP/dt. At P = 1000, 1 − 2P/5000 = 0.6 > 0 and dP/dt = 0.4(1000)(0.8) = 320 > 0, so d²P/dt² > 0: concave up. Below 2500 fish the growth is speeding up.
  4. (d) 5000/(1 + 24e−0.4t) = 2500 gives 24e−0.4t = 1, so t = ln 24 / 0.4 ≈ 7.945 years. This is the inflection point, the steepest part of the S.
  5. Why fisheries care (beyond the AP exam). If H fish per year are caught, dP/dt = 0.4P(1 − P/5000) − H. The population can hold steady only if H is at most the largest natural growth rate, 500 per year, which happens when the lake holds 2500 fish. This is the idea of a "maximum sustainable yield".

Common mistakes

  • Using L = the limit of dP/dt. L is the limit of P itself.
  • Reading the maximum growth at P = L instead of L/2.
  • Forgetting the sign: for P > L the population decreases.
  • Confusing logistic with exponential growth.
  • Mixing up P (population) and dP/dt (growth rate) when reading graphs.
  • For concavity, forgetting the chain rule: d²P/dt² needs dP/dt as a factor.

Solving it

Separating variables with partial fractions gives P(t) = L/(1 + Ae−kt) with A = (L − P₀)/P₀. You rarely need the formula on the exam: the equation itself tells you the limit, the equilibria and where growth is fastest.
