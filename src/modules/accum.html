Accumulation & Riemann sums

How much water flowed into the tank? Add up the rate over time. When the rate changes, slice the time into short pieces and add rate × time for each.

The idea

If water flows at a constant 5 gallons per minute for 10 minutes, the amount is 5 × 10 = 50 gallons: rate × time, which is the area of a rectangle on a graph of rate against time. When the rate changes, slice the time into thin strips, treat the rate as nearly constant on each strip, and add up the rectangle areas. That is a Riemann sum.

Thinner strips give a better answer. In the limit as the number of strips goes to infinity the sum becomes the definite integral ∫ab f(x) dx: the net accumulation of f from a to b, with area above the axis counted positive and below counted negative. Left, right, midpoint and trapezoid sums choose the height differently.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. On x² with left sums and n = 4, is the estimate too big or too small?

    What you should see

    Too small: x² is increasing, so the left edge of each strip is the lowest point and every rectangle sits under the curve. Right sums would overestimate.

  2. Increase n to 60. What happens to the error?

    What you should see

    It shrinks toward 0: the sum approaches the integral as n → ∞. The error of a left or right sum falls roughly in proportion to 1/n.

  3. Switch to the trapezoid rule on x² with n = 4. Over or under?

    What you should see

    Over: 9.28125 against the exact 9. x² is concave up, so each straight top edge lies above the curve.

  4. Choose 4 − x² with the midpoint rule and n = 60. Slide b from 2 up to 3. Why does the total go down?

    What you should see

    Up to b = 2 the total is 16/3 ≈ 5.33, the largest it gets. After x = 2 the rate is negative (pink strips), so the amount decreases, reaching 3 at b = 3. Think of water draining out once the flow reverses.

  5. Choose sin x and slide b all the way to 2π ≈ 6.28. What is the exact integral now?

    What you should see

    0: the gold area above the axis on [0, π] (2) is cancelled by the pink area below it on [π, 2π] (−2). A definite integral is net change, not total area.

Worked example

Use a left Riemann sum with n = 3 to estimate ∫03 x² dx.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Strip width. Δx = (3 − 0)/3 = 1.
  2. Left endpoints. x = 0, 1, 2, so f = 0, 1, 4.
  3. Add rate × width. L₃ = (0 + 1 + 4)(1) = 5.
  4. Compare. The exact value is 9, so L₃ is an underestimate (f is increasing).
  5. Answer. 5. Right sum: (1 + 4 + 9)(1) = 14, an overestimate.

Worked example 2: a car's speedometer (AP-style table)

A car's speed v(t) is read from its speedometer at the times in the table. (a) Use a left Riemann sum with the four subintervals in the table to estimate ∫030 v(t) dt, and explain its meaning with units. (b) Estimate the same integral with a trapezoidal sum. (c) Is the left sum an overestimate or an underestimate?

t (seconds)010152530
v(t) (meters per second)1218211714

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Widths. The readings are not evenly spaced: the strips are 10, 5, 10 and 5 seconds wide. Use each width separately, not one Δx.
  2. (a) Left sum. Use the speed at the start of each strip: 12(10) + 18(5) + 21(10) + 17(5) = 120 + 90 + 210 + 85 = 505.
  3. Meaning. ∫030 v(t) dt is the car's change in position over the 30 seconds. The speed is positive, so the car traveled about 505 meters in those 30 seconds. Units: (m/s) × s = m.
  4. (b) Trapezoids. Average the two ends of each strip: 10(12 + 18)/2 + 5(18 + 21)/2 + 10(21 + 17)/2 + 5(17 + 14)/2 = 150 + 97.5 + 190 + 77.5 = 515 meters.
  5. (c) Over or under? It cannot be determined. A left sum is low where v increases and high where it decreases, and these readings rise then fall. Without knowing v is monotonic on the whole interval, you cannot say.

Common mistakes

  • Using the wrong number of heights: n strips use n heights, not n + 1 (except in the trapezoid rule's endpoints).
  • Using Δx = b − a instead of (b − a)/n.
  • With a table of uneven times, using one Δx for every strip.
  • Calling a left sum an overestimate for an increasing function. It underestimates.
  • Forgetting that area below the axis counts negative in a definite integral.
  • Dropping units: (liters/minute) × minutes = liters.

Which sum is high or low?

For an increasing function, left sums underestimate and right sums overestimate; for a decreasing function it reverses. The trapezoid rule overestimates when f is concave up (the chords lie above the curve) and underestimates when concave down; the midpoint rule does the opposite. Averaging left and right sums gives the trapezoid sum.
