Related rates

When two quantities are tied together by an equation and both change in time, their rates are tied together too.

The idea

A ladder slides down a wall. The foot moves out at a steady 2 ft/s, but the top does not move down at a steady rate: it starts slowly and speeds up. The lengths x and y are linked by x² + y² = L², so their rates are linked by the derivative of that equation with respect to time.

The method: draw and label; write an equation that holds at every moment; differentiate with respect to t (chain rule on every variable); only then substitute the numbers of the moment you care about. Substituting before differentiating freezes the variables and gives 0.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. On the ladder (10 ft, foot moving out at 2 ft/s) set x = 6. How fast is the top sliding down?

    What you should see

    y = 8, and dy/dt = −(x/y)(2) = −(6/8)(2) = −1.5 ft/s: the top drops 1.5 ft/s.

  2. Now slide x up to 9. What happens to the speed of the top?

    What you should see

    y ≈ 4.36 and dy/dt ≈ −4.13 ft/s: it speeds up a lot as the ladder nears the floor, even though the foot moves at a steady 2 ft/s.

  3. Keep x = 6 and double the given rate to 4 ft/s. Predict the new dy/dt before you look.

    What you should see

    dy/dt = −(6/8)(4) = −3 ft/s, exactly double. At a fixed moment the rate you want is proportional to the given rate; only the moment (x and y) sets the factor.

  4. Choose the lamppost. Drag the person from 5 ft to 25 ft from the pole. Does the shadow grow faster when the person is far away?

    What you should see

    No. The shadow grows at 10/3 ≈ 3.33 ft/s and its tip moves at 25/3 ≈ 8.33 ft/s wherever the person is, because similar triangles make s = (2/3)x, a straight-line relationship.

  5. Choose the cone tank (water in at 2 ft³/min). Compare dh/dt at h = 2 ft and at h = 8 ft.

    What you should see

    About 0.995 ft/min at h = 2 but only 0.062 ft/min at h = 8, 16 times slower: the surface is 4 times wider, so its area is 16 times bigger, and the same water spreads over it.

Worked example

A 10 ft ladder leans on a wall. Its foot slides away at 2 ft/s. How fast is the top sliding down when the foot is 6 ft from the wall?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Draw and equation. x = distance of foot from wall, y = height of top: x² + y² = 100.
  2. Differentiate with respect to t. 2x·dx/dt + 2y·dy/dt = 0, so dy/dt = −(x/y)·dx/dt.
  3. Substitute the moment. x = 6 gives y = √(100 − 36) = 8, and dx/dt = 2.
  4. Compute. dy/dt = −(6/8)(2) = −1.5.
  5. Answer. The top is sliding down at 1.5 ft/s (dy/dt = −1.5 ft/s).

Worked example 2: a shadow on the sidewalk

A street light is at the top of a 15 ft pole. A person 6 ft tall walks away from the pole at 5 ft/s. (a) How fast is the person's shadow getting longer when they are 40 ft from the pole? (b) How fast is the tip of the shadow moving?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Draw and name. x = distance from the pole to the person, s = length of the shadow. The tip of the shadow is x + s from the pole. Given: dx/dt = 5 ft/s.
  2. Equation that holds at every moment. The big triangle (pole, ground to the tip) and the small one (person, shadow) are similar: 15/(x + s) = 6/s. Cross-multiply: 15s = 6x + 6s, so 9s = 6x and s = (2/3)x.
  3. (a) Differentiate in t. ds/dt = (2/3)·dx/dt = (2/3)(5) = 10/3 ≈ 3.33 ft/s.
  4. (b) The tip. The tip is at D = x + s = (5/3)x, so dD/dt = (5/3)(5) = 25/3 ≈ 8.33 ft/s. The tip outruns the person because the shadow is growing too.
  5. What about the 40 ft? Neither answer depends on x: s is a constant multiple of x, so its rate is too. AP problems sometimes give a value you do not need; say so rather than forcing it in.

Common mistakes

  • Substituting numbers before differentiating. Constants have derivative 0, so you lose the rates.
  • Forgetting dx/dt: 2x alone is not d/dt of x².
  • Treating a changing quantity as constant (for example the ladder's length is constant, but x and y are not).
  • Ignoring signs: a decreasing quantity has a negative rate. Report it with the right direction.
  • Dropping units. Rates have units of quantity per time.
  • In shadow problems, using the distance to the person when the question asks about the tip of the shadow (x + s).

A checklist

Name every variable with units. Write what is constant and what changes. Use a relationship that is true at all times (Pythagoras, similar triangles, a volume or area formula), not just at the moment asked about. Differentiate. Substitute known values. Solve. Include units and a sentence.
