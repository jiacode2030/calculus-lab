Polar curves & area

Describe a point by its distance r from the origin and its angle θ. Flowers, spirals and hearts have simple polar equations.

The idea

A point has polar coordinates (r, θ) and rectangular coordinates x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ. A polar curve r = f(θ) draws a point at distance f(θ) in direction θ as θ sweeps. The cardioid r = 1 + cos θ, the rose r = 2 cos 2θ and the spiral r = θ are all simple in polar form and awkward in rectangular form.

The area swept out is made of thin sectors (pizza slices): a sector of angle dθ and radius r has area ½r² dθ, so A = ½ ∫αβ r² dθ. The slope is found as for a parametric curve (x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ), giving dy/dx = (r′ sin θ + r cos θ)/(r′ cos θ − r sin θ).

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. On the cardioid, sweep θ from 0 to π. What fraction of the total area is swept?

    What you should see

    Half: by symmetry across the x-axis, the upper half of the cardioid is half the area, 3π/4 of 3π/2.

  2. On the rose r = 2 cos 2θ, where is r negative?

    What you should see

    When cos 2θ < 0, for π/4 < θ < 3π/4: the point is plotted in the opposite direction, which draws the other petals.

  3. On the rose, set θ = 6.28 (a full turn). What is the area swept, and what does it cover?

    What you should see

    The total is 2π ≈ 6.283: ½ ∫ 4cos²2θ dθ over 0 to 2π. The four petals each have area π/2, and the sectors for negative r still add area, because r² is never negative.

  4. Choose the limaçon r = 1 + 2 cos θ and set θ = 3.14. What is r, and where is the point?

    What you should see

    r = 1 + 2 cos π = −1, so the point is plotted one unit in the direction opposite to θ = π, that is at (1, 0) on the positive x-axis. This is the tip of the small inner loop, which exists only where r is negative.

  5. Choose the circle r = 2 sin θ and set θ = π/2 (about 1.57). How much area is swept, and when is the circle finished?

    What you should see

    Half of π: the area swept is about 1.57 (π/2), and the circle is complete at θ = π, with total area π. This circle has radius 1, so π is right, and θ only needs to run from 0 to π, not 2π.

Worked example

Find the area enclosed by the cardioid r = 1 + cos θ.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Formula. A = ½ ∫02π r² dθ = ½ ∫02π (1 + cos θ)² dθ.
  2. Expand. (1 + cos θ)² = 1 + 2 cos θ + cos²θ = 3/2 + 2 cos θ + (1/2)cos 2θ.
  3. Integrate. ∫02π (3/2 + 2 cos θ + (1/2)cos 2θ) dθ = (3/2)(2π) = 3π (the cosines integrate to 0).
  4. Answer. A = (1/2)(3π) = 3π/2.

Worked example 2: a microphone's pickup region (AP-style model)

A cardioid microphone sits at the origin facing the positive x-axis. Model the region in which it picks up a performer clearly by r = 4(1 + cos θ), with r in feet and θ measured from the direction the microphone faces. (a) Find the area of the region. (b) Find how far the pickup reaches directly ahead, directly to the side (θ = π/2) and directly behind. (c) Find the area of the part behind the microphone, π/2 ≤ θ ≤ 3π/2, and what fraction of the whole it is.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. (a) Area = ½ ∫02π [4(1 + cos θ)]² dθ = 16 · ½ ∫02π (1 + cos θ)² dθ = 16 · (3π/2) = 24π ≈ 75.40 ft², using the result of the first example.
  2. (b) r(0) = 4(2) = 8 ft ahead, r(π/2) = 4(1) = 4 ft to the side, and r(π) = 4(0) = 0 ft directly behind.
  3. (c) Rear half. A = ½ ∫π/23π/2 16(1 + cos θ)² dθ = 8 [3θ/2 + 2 sin θ + (1/4) sin 2θ]π/23π/2 = 8(3π/2 − 4) = 12π − 32 ≈ 5.70 ft².
  4. Fraction. 5.70 ÷ 75.40 ≈ 7.6%: only about one thirteenth of the pickup region lies behind the microphone.
  5. Why microphones are built this way (beyond the AP exam). A cardioid pattern rejects sound from behind, which is why such microphones are used on stage next to loudspeakers. Real patterns describe sensitivity rather than a sharp edge, so this region is only a model.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the ½ in the area formula.
  • Using ∫ r dθ instead of ∫ r² dθ.
  • Using the wrong interval for θ so that the curve is traced twice or only partly.
  • Mixing up (r, θ) and (x, y) in conversions.
  • Treating a negative r as an error: it simply plots the point on the opposite side.

Finding limits

To get the right θ limits, find where r = 0 (the curve returns to the origin) for one petal or loop, or use symmetry. Between two curves r₁ and r₂ (r₁ outer): A = ½ ∫ (r₁² − r₂²) dθ.
