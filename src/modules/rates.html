Average vs instantaneous rate

Speed over a trip is easy: distance divided by time. Speed at one instant needs a limit. Watch a secant line turn into a tangent line.

The idea

The average rate of change of f from x = a to x = b is the slope of the secant line through the two points: (f(b) − f(a)) / (b − a). It is what a car's trip computer tells you: miles per hour over the whole trip.

The instantaneous rate of change is what the speedometer shows at one moment. Shrink the interval: move the second point toward the first. The secant line rotates and settles into the tangent line, and its slope is the limit of the average rates. That limit is the derivative.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Make h smaller and smaller on x² at a = 1. What do the secant slopes approach?

    What you should see

    Toward 2, the slope of the tangent line at x = 1. With h = 0.05 the average rate is 2.05; with h = 0.001 it would be 2.001.

  2. Set h negative (second point on the left). Does the answer still approach the same number?

    What you should see

    Yes: 1.95 from the left, 2.05 from the right. For a smooth curve both sides squeeze toward the same tangent slope.

  3. Choose eˣ at a = 0 with h = 1.2, then shrink h to 0.05. What number is the secant slope heading for?

    What you should see

    About 1.93 at h = 1.2 and 1.03 at h = 0.05, heading for 1: the slope of eˣ at 0 is e⁰ = 1. The big gap overestimates because eˣ curves upward.

  4. Choose sin x with a = 1.6 (near the top of the hump). Compare h = 0.5 with h = −0.5.

    What you should see

    About −0.27 to the right and +0.22 to the left: the two secants tilt opposite ways because they straddle the peak. The tangent slope, cos 1.6 ≈ −0.03, is nearly flat and sits between them.

Worked example

A car's position is s(t) = t² feet after t seconds. Find the average velocity from t = 1 to t = 3 and estimate the velocity at t = 1.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Average rate. (s(3) − s(1))/(3 − 1) = (9 − 1)/2 = 4 ft/s.
  2. Shrink the interval. From t = 1 to t = 1.1: (1.21 − 1)/0.1 = 2.1 ft/s. From 1 to 1.01: (1.0201 − 1)/0.01 = 2.01.
  3. Spot the pattern. The average velocities approach 2.
  4. Answer. Average velocity on [1, 3] is 4 ft/s; the instantaneous velocity at t = 1 is 2 ft/s.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. (a) Whole trip. (D(2) − D(0))/(2 − 0) = 120/2 = 60 mph. That is the slope of the long dashed secant.
  2. (b) Just before t = 1. (D(1) − D(0.5))/(1 − 0.5) = 33/0.5 = 66 mph.
  3. Just after t = 1. (D(1.5) − D(1))/(1.5 − 1) = 24/0.5 = 48 mph. The two one-sided averages disagree a lot: the car was slowing down around t = 1.
  4. (c) Center the interval. (D(1.5) − D(0.5))/(1.5 − 0.5) = 57/1 = 57 mph. It uses readings on both sides of t = 1, so it is the average of 66 and 48 and the best estimate from this table.
  5. Meaning. D′(1) ≈ 57 means that at t = 1 hour the speedometer read about 57 miles per hour. Units: miles per hour, because D is in miles and t in hours.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing by the wrong thing: the denominator is b − a (change in input), not b.
  • Forgetting units. A rate has units of output per input, like ft/s or dollars per item.
  • Thinking the average rate over an interval equals the rate at its midpoint. It only does for special functions.
  • Using a gap h = 0 in the formula: the quotient is 0/0. A limit, not substitution.
  • With a table, using readings that do not contain the time asked about.

Two ways to see it

Geometrically: secant slope → tangent slope. Numerically: a table of (f(a+h) − f(a))/h for smaller and smaller h. Algebraically: simplify the quotient, then let h → 0. The next module does that last step formally.
