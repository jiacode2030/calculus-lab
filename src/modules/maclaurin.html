Maclaurin series & operations

A few series to know by heart, and a toolbox for building new ones: substitute, multiply, differentiate, integrate.

The idea

The key series (centered at 0): eˣ = Σ xⁿ/n! (all x), sin x = Σ (−1)ⁿ x²ⁿ⁺¹/(2n + 1)! (all x), cos x = Σ (−1)ⁿ x²ⁿ/(2n)! (all x), and 1/(1 − x) = Σ xⁿ (|x| < 1). Everything else can be made from these.

Substitute: replace x by 2x or x²: e^{−x²} = Σ (−1)ⁿ x²ⁿ/n!. Multiply by x or a power. Differentiate or integrate term by term: integrating 1/(1 + x) gives ln(1 + x) = Σ (−1)ⁿ⁺¹ xⁿ/n; integrating 1/(1 + x²) gives arctan x = Σ (−1)ⁿ x²ⁿ⁺¹/(2n + 1). Series can also evaluate limits and integrals that have no closed form.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Choose e^(−x²). Where does the series come from, and how many terms are needed near x = 1?

    What you should see

    Replace x by −x² in the series for eˣ: 1 − x² + x⁴/2 − x⁶/6 + …. With 4 terms the curve matches well for |x| ≤ 1.3 or so.

  2. Choose arctan x and increase the terms. What happens past |x| = 1?

    What you should see

    The partial sums fly off: the series has radius 1. It was obtained by integrating Σ (−1)ⁿ x²ⁿ = 1/(1 + x²), which only converges for |x| < 1.

  3. Choose sin x. With 3 terms, how close is the curve at x = 2? And with 4?

    What you should see

    With 3 terms (x − x³/6 + x⁵/120) the value at x = 2 is 0.9333 against sin 2 = 0.9093, an error of about 0.024; with 4 terms it is 0.9079, an error of about 0.0014. Each added term widens the window where the polynomial hugs the curve.

  4. Choose ln(1 + x) with 6 terms. Compare x = 0.5 with x = 2.

    What you should see

    Near x = 0.5 the partial sum is excellent, but at x = 2 it gives −5.6 while ln 3 ≈ 1.0986: the partial sums fly off past x = 1. The radius is 1, because it came from integrating 1/(1 + x) = Σ (−x)ⁿ, a geometric series.

  5. Choose sin(x²) and use 2 terms. Where does it follow the curve, and how is this series built?

    What you should see

    Replace x by x² in the sin series: x² − x⁶/6 + …. Two terms follow the curve well for |x| up to about 1 and then drift. No derivatives were computed: substitution gave every coefficient at once.

Worked example

Use known series to find the first three nonzero terms of x·sin(x²) and limx→0 (ex − 1 − x)/x².

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Substitute. sin u = u − u³/6 + u⁵/120 − …, with u = x²: sin(x²) = x² − x⁶/6 + x¹⁰/120 − ….
  2. Multiply by x. x sin(x²) = x³ − x⁷/6 + x¹¹/120 − ….
  3. Limit. eˣ − 1 − x = x²/2 + x³/6 + …, so (eˣ − 1 − x)/x² = 1/2 + x/6 + … → 1/2.
  4. Answer. x³ − x⁷/6 + x¹¹/120; the limit is 1/2.

Common mistakes

  • Not replacing x consistently when substituting (every x in the series changes).
  • Forgetting factorials in ex, sin and cos.
  • Using the sin series for cos (or the reverse): sin has odd powers, cos has even powers.
  • Forgetting the alternating sign (−1)ⁿ.
  • Ignoring the interval of convergence after substituting or integrating.

Why these are enough

Differentiating the series for sin x term by term gives the series for cos x, and integrating Σ xⁿ gives −ln(1 − x). The AP exam usually asks for the first few terms, a pattern (general term), or a value computed from a known series, and the error bound.
