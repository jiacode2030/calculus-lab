Volumes from cross sections

Stack thin slices of a known shape along an axis, and add up their volumes. A slice with area A(x) and thickness dx has volume A(x) dx.

The idea

A solid is built on a base region in the xy-plane. Cut it by planes perpendicular to the x-axis: each cut is a shape (a square, a semicircle, an equilateral triangle, a rectangle) whose size depends on the base length s(x) = top − bottom. Compute its area A(x), and the volume is V = ∫ab A(x) dx.

Areas to know, with s the base length: square A = s²; semicircle A = (π/8)s² (the diameter is s); equilateral triangle A = (√3/4)s²; rectangle of height h A = h·s; isosceles right triangle with leg s A = s²/2. The hard part is only getting s(x) right.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Choose squares. Where is the slice largest?

    What you should see

    At x = 0.5: s = 0.25 is the longest base, so the square slice is largest there. V = ∫(x − x²)² dx = 1/30.

  2. Switch to semicircles. How does the volume compare with squares?

    What you should see

    π/8 ≈ 0.39 of the square volume (about 0.0131): a semicircle with diameter s covers π/8 of the square of side s.

  3. Switch to equilateral triangles. Predict the volume before you look.

    What you should see

    (√3/4)(1/30) ≈ 0.0144: every slice is √3/4 ≈ 0.433 of the square on the same base, so the whole volume is too.

  4. Compare the slices at x = 0.1 and x = 0.9.

    What you should see

    Both have s = 0.09: x − x² is symmetric about x = 0.5, so the solid is too. Area A(x) only depends on the base length, not on where the slice sits.

Worked example

The base of a solid is the region between y = x² and y = x. Cross sections perpendicular to the x-axis are squares. Find the volume.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Base length. s(x) = x − x² on [0, 1].
  2. Area of a square slice. A(x) = s² = (x − x²)² = x² − 2x³ + x⁴.
  3. Integrate. V = ∫01 (x² − 2x³ + x⁴) dx = 1/3 − 1/2 + 1/5.
  4. Stack them. Every thin square of area A(x) and thickness dx contributes A(x) dx; the integral adds them up.
  5. Answer. 1/30 ≈ 0.0333.

Worked example 2: the Great Pyramid

The Great Pyramid of Giza had a square base about 230 m on a side and was about 147 m tall. (a) Using horizontal square cross sections, find its volume. (b) Compare it with a box that has the same base and height. (c) At what height is half of the pyramid's volume below you?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Slice horizontally. At height y (in m), the cross section is a square whose side shrinks in a straight line from 230 at the base to 0 at the top: s(y) = 230(1 − y/147).
  2. Area of a slice. A(y) = s(y)² = 230²(1 − y/147)².
  3. (a) Integrate. V = ∫0147 230²(1 − y/147)² dy. With u = 1 − y/147 (dy = −147 du) this is 230²·147·∫01 u² du = 230²·147/3 = 2,592,100 m³, about 2.6 million cubic meters.
  4. (b) Compare. The box is 230²·147 = 7,776,300 m³, so the pyramid is exactly 1/3 of it. That is where V = (1/3)(base area)(height) for every pyramid and cone comes from.
  5. (c) Half the volume. Above height y sits a smaller pyramid scaled by (1 − y/147), with volume V(1 − y/147)³. Set it to V/2: 1 − y/147 = 2−1/3, so y = 147(1 − 2−1/3) ≈ 30.3 m. Half of all the stone is in the bottom fifth of the height.

Common mistakes

  • Using the wrong base length: it is top − bottom, not just one function.
  • Squaring after integrating instead of before: A(x) must be squared first.
  • Using the radius instead of the diameter for a semicircle (the diameter is s).
  • Forgetting the constant in the area formula (π/8, √3/4).
  • Mixing dx and dy slices: the slice direction decides which variable you integrate.

Derivations of the area formulas

Semicircle with diameter s: radius s/2, area (1/2)π(s/2)² = πs²/8. Equilateral triangle with side s: area (1/2)·s·(√3/2)s = (√3/4)s². Square: s². Keep these in your head: they are the whole difficulty in most cross-section problems.
