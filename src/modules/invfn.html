Inverse functions & inverse trig

If f turns a into b, its inverse turns b back into a. Their slopes at matching points are reciprocals, and that fact gives the derivatives of ln, arcsin and arctan.

The idea

The graph of f⁻¹ is the graph of f reflected over the line y = x. A reflection swaps the roles of rise and run, so a tangent of slope m at (a, b) becomes a tangent of slope 1/m at (b, a). That is the rule: (f⁻¹)′(b) = 1 / f′(a) where f(a) = b.

Use it on f(x) = sin x to get (arcsin x)′ = 1/√(1 − x²), and on tan x to get (arctan x)′ = 1/(1 + x²). The AP course asks for arcsin, arccos and arctan.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. On eˣ, find the product f′(a)·(f⁻¹)′.

    What you should see

    Always 1: the slopes are reciprocals. At a = 0.6, f′ = e^0.6 ≈ 1.82, and the inverse (ln) has slope 1/1.82 ≈ 0.55 at the matching point.

  2. On arcsin x, move x toward 1. What happens to the slope?

    What you should see

    It grows large: arcsin has a vertical tangent at x = 1, matching 1/√(1 − x²) → ∞. The sine curve is flat there, so its inverse is steep.

Worked example

If f(x) = x³ + x, find (f⁻¹)′(10).

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Find a. We need f(a) = 10. Try a = 2: 8 + 2 = 10. ✓
  2. Differentiate f. f′(x) = 3x² + 1, so f′(2) = 13.
  3. Reciprocal. (f⁻¹)′(10) = 1/f′(2).
  4. Answer. 1/13.

Common mistakes

  • Evaluating f′ at b instead of at a. You need the x that f sends to b.
  • Writing (f⁻¹)′ = 1/f′(x) without the inverse in the argument: it is 1/f′(f⁻¹(b)).
  • Confusing f⁻¹ with 1/f.
  • Forgetting the domain restrictions for inverse trig.
  • Dropping the chain rule: d/dx arctan(2x) = 2/(1 + 4x²), not 1/(1 + 4x²).

Why arcsin has that derivative

Let y = arcsin x, so sin y = x. Differentiate implicitly: cos y · y′ = 1, so y′ = 1/cos y. With y between −π/2 and π/2, cos y = √(1 − sin²y) = √(1 − x²).
