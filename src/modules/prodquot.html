Product & quotient rules

The derivative of a product is not the product of the derivatives. Here is what it is, and why the naive answer fails.

The idea

If f = u·v, change in f has two parts: u changes while v stays, and v changes while u stays. That gives (uv)′ = u′v + uv′: "derivative of the first times the second, plus the first times the derivative of the second". For a quotient, (u/v)′ = (u′v − uv′)/v²: low d-high minus high d-low over low squared.

You can check that (uv)′ ≠ u′v′ with any example: for u = v = x, uv = x² has derivative 2x, but u′v′ = 1. Watch the lab: the naive answer drifts away from the true slope.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Choose x·eˣ at x = 1.5. Compare the three numbers.

    What you should see

    The product rule matches the numeric slope (about 11.2); the naive u′v′ gives e1.5 ≈ 4.48. Only the product rule is right.

  2. Choose the quotient sin x / x and compare.

    What you should see

    (x cos x − sin x)/x² agrees with the numeric slope. Dividing the derivatives (cos x / 1) does not.

Worked example

Differentiate f(x) = x² eˣ and g(x) = (x + 1)/(x − 2).

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Product rule on f. u = x², v = eˣ, so u′ = 2x, v′ = eˣ. f′ = 2x·eˣ + x²·eˣ = eˣ(x² + 2x).
  2. Quotient rule on g. u = x + 1, v = x − 2, so u′ = 1, v′ = 1. g′ = (1·(x − 2) − (x + 1)·1)/(x − 2)².
  3. Simplify the top. (x − 2) − (x + 1) = −3.
  4. Answer. f′(x) = eˣ(x² + 2x) and g′(x) = −3/(x − 2)².

Common mistakes

  • Writing (uv)′ = u′v′. It misses half of the change.
  • Getting the quotient numerator in the wrong order: it is u′v − uv′ (low d-high minus high d-low).
  • Forgetting to square the denominator.
  • Using the quotient rule when the denominator is a constant. Just pull the constant out.
  • Not simplifying: factor and cancel if you can.

Why the product rule has two terms

A rectangle with sides u and v has area uv. If both sides grow, the area gains a strip along each side: u′v from stretching one side and uv′ from stretching the other. The tiny corner u′v′ is second order and vanishes in the limit.
