The chain rule

When one function sits inside another, rates multiply: the speed of the outer function times the speed of the inner one.

The idea

Picture a chain of gears. If gear A turns 3 times as fast as gear B, and gear B turns twice as fast as gear C, then A turns 3 × 2 = 6 times as fast as C. A composite function y = f(g(x)) works the same way: as x changes, u = g(x) changes at rate g′(x), and y changes with u at rate f′(u). Multiply them.

So d/dx f(g(x)) = f′(g(x)) · g′(x): differentiate the outside, leave the inside alone, then multiply by the derivative of the inside. Leibniz writes it so it looks like cancelling: dy/dx = (dy/du)(du/dx).

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Choose e²ˣ. What are the two rates at any x, and what do they give?

    What you should see

    The inner rate is always 2, the outer rate is e2x: the product 2e2x matches the numeric slope. The factor 2 is what the chain rule adds.

  2. Choose (3x+1)⁴ and change x. Which factor is always the same?

    What you should see

    The inner rate 3. The outer rate 4u³ depends on x, but the inner rate does not, so the derivative is 4(3x+1)³·3 = 12(3x + 1)³.

Worked example

Differentiate f(x) = sin(x²) and g(x) = (3x + 1)⁵.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Name the layers. For f: outer = sin u, inner = u = x². For g: outer = u⁵, inner = u = 3x + 1.
  2. Differentiate the outside, keep the inside. f′ = cos(x²) · (x²)′. g′ = 5(3x + 1)⁴ · (3x + 1)′.
  3. Multiply by the derivative of the inside. (x²)′ = 2x and (3x + 1)′ = 3.
  4. Answer. f′(x) = 2x cos(x²); g′(x) = 15(3x + 1)⁴.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the inside derivative: (sin x²)′ is not cos x².
  • Changing the inside when differentiating the outside. cos(x²) keeps x² as it is.
  • Applying the power rule to eˣ-type functions: (e3x)′ = 3e3x, not 3x e3x−1.
  • Stopping early on layers: √(sin(x²)) has three layers.
  • Mixing up the order of composition when evaluating at a point: you need g(a) first, then f′(g(a)).
