Power, sum & constant-multiple rules

You will not use the limit definition every time. A few rules let you differentiate polynomials, roots and reciprocals almost instantly.

The idea

Power rule: d/dx [xn] = n xn−1. Bring the exponent down in front and lower the exponent by one. It works for every real n: x³, √x = x1/2, and 1/x² = x−2.

Constants and sums: constants multiply through, (c·f)′ = c·f′, and sums differentiate term by term, (f + g)′ = f′ + g′. The derivative of a constant is 0, because a flat line has slope 0. Rewrite roots and fractions as powers first.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Set n = 2 and x = 1.5. What are the formula and the graph's slope?

    What you should see

    f′(x) = 2x, so the slope at 1.5 is 3. The numeric slope agrees: the rule is the shortcut for the limit.

  2. Set n = 0.5 (a square root). What is f′ at x = 1?

    What you should see

    f′(x) = ½x−1/2 = 1/(2√x), so f′(1) = 0.5. The square root flattens as x grows.

Worked example

Differentiate f(x) = 4x³ − 6√x + 5/x² + 7.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Rewrite as powers. f(x) = 4x³ − 6x1/2 + 5x−2 + 7.
  2. Differentiate each term. 4·3x² = 12x²; −6·(1/2)x−1/2 = −3x−1/2; 5·(−2)x−3 = −10x−3; the constant 7 goes to 0.
  3. Answer. f′(x) = 12x² − 3x−1/2 − 10x−3, or 12x² − 3/√x − 10/x³.

Common mistakes

  • Differentiating a constant as if it had an x: the derivative of 7 is 0, and of 7x is 7.
  • Forgetting to lower the exponent, writing (x³)′ = 3x³.
  • Applying the power rule to eˣ or 2ˣ. The variable is in the exponent there: a different rule.
  • Not rewriting 1/x² or √x as powers before differentiating.
  • Splitting a product or quotient term by term. (x²·x³)′ is not (x²)′·(x³)′.

Why it works

The derivative of x² is 2x (you saw it from the definition). For x³ expand (x + h)³ = x³ + 3x²h + 3xh² + h³, subtract x³, divide by h and let h → 0: 3x². The pattern n xn−1 comes from the binomial expansion, and it holds for every real n.
