    Functions & transformations

    Before derivatives: how to read a formula as a graph. Shift, stretch and flip a parent function and see exactly what each number does.

    The idea

    Every graph in calculus is a familiar parent function that has been moved. y = a·f(b(x − h)) + k says: shift right by h, shift up by k, stretch vertically by a (flip if negative) and squeeze horizontally by b. Changes inside the parentheses act on x and act backwards (x − h moves right); changes outside act on y and do what they say.

    Limits from graphs & tables

    A limit asks where a function is heading as x gets close to a number, not what the function does at that number. That one change lets calculus talk about a tangent line, a speed at an instant, and an area under a curve.

    The idea

    Walk toward a doorway along a hallway whose floor is the graph of f. As you get closer to the doorway at x = a, your height settles toward some value L. That height is the limit. It does not matter whether there is a floor tile exactly at the doorway, or whether the tile there is at a different height. The limit only cares about the approach.

    You can approach from the left or from the right. The limit exists only when both approaches head to the same height. If the floor has a step in it, the two sides disagree and the limit does not exist.

    Read a limit from a graph

    Open circle = the graph does not include that point. Filled dot = the graph's value there. Tap a question to see what the graph says, then make a new graph.

    Worked example

    Estimate limx→2 (x² − 4)/(x − 2) from a table, then say why plugging in fails.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Plug in. At x = 2 the expression is 0/0, which tells us nothing. The function has no value at 2, so we look at nearby values.
    2. Left side. f(1.9) = (3.61 − 4)/(−0.1) = 3.9, f(1.99) = 3.99, f(1.999) = 3.999.
    3. Right side. f(2.1) = (4.41 − 4)/0.1 = 4.1, f(2.01) = 4.01, f(2.001) = 4.001.
    4. Compare. Both sides head toward 4, from below on the left and above on the right. So limx→2 (x² − 4)/(x − 2) = 4.
    5. Check with a graph. (x² − 4)/(x − 2) = x + 2 everywhere except x = 2, so its graph is the line y = x + 2 with a hole at (2, 4). A table gives a good estimate; the algebra in the next module will prove it.

    Common mistakes

    • Thinking the limit equals f(a). It often does (for smooth graphs), but it need not: the limit ignores the value at a.
    • Saying a limit "doesn't exist" because f(a) has no value. A hole still has a limit.
    • Reading a graph's limit from the filled dot instead of from the heights the two sides approach.
    • Believing a table proves a limit. It gives a strong estimate; oscillating functions can fool a short table.
    • Writing "lim = 3 and −1". If the two sides disagree, the two-sided limit does not exist (DNE); the one-sided limits are separate answers.

    When a limit fails to exist

    There are three common ways. A jump: the one-sided limits are both numbers but unequal. Unbounded growth: the function blows up toward ∞ or −∞ near a (the next modules call this an infinite limit; the AP exam says the limit does not exist but may describe it as ∞). Oscillation: the function keeps swinging without settling, like sin(1/x) at 0.

    The formal definition (every tolerance around L is met once x is close enough to a) is what makes this rigorous. AP Calculus works with the idea rather than the proof, but it is the reason limits can be added, multiplied and divided safely, which the next module uses.

    Algebraic limits

    When plugging in gives 0/0, the function is hiding something. Algebra rewrites it into a form that does not hide it.

    The idea

    Continuity & the IVT

    A function is continuous if you can draw it without lifting your pencil. Calculus needs a sharper test, and one big consequence: continuous functions cannot skip values.

    The idea

    Continuity at a point means three things hold: f(a) exists, the limit as x → a exists, and they are equal. A hole, a jump or a blow-up each breaks one of the three. Continuous on an interval means continuous at every point of it.

    If a function is continuous on [a, b] it cannot skip over any value between f(a) and f(b). That is the Intermediate Value Theorem. It is why you can say a root exists in an interval without finding it.

    Two guarantees about continuous and differentiable functions: there is always a highest and lowest point, and somewhere the slope equals the average slope.

    Accumulation & Riemann sums

    How much water flowed into the tank? Add up the rate over time. When the rate changes, slice the time into short pieces and add rate × time for each.

    The idea

    If water flows at a constant 5 gallons per minute for 10 minutes, the amount is 5 × 10 = 50 gallons: rate × time, which is the area of a rectangle on a graph of rate against time. When the rate changes, slice the time into thin strips, treat the rate as nearly constant on each strip, and add up the rectangle areas. That is a Riemann sum.

    Thinner strips give a better answer. In the limit as the number of strips goes to infinity the sum becomes the definite integral ∫ab f(x) dx: the net accumulation of f from a to b, with area above the axis counted positive and below counted negative. Left, right, midpoint and trapezoid sums choose the height differently.

    An antiderivative undoes a derivative. Because constants vanish when you differentiate, every function has a whole family of antiderivatives.

    The idea
